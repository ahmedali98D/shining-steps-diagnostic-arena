2.2 Frameworks: what each is actually for — and what the evidence says

Your Section 4 lists nine frameworks. Here is the honest adjudication:

Load-bearing (architecture-level) — keep four:

    CEFR (2020 Companion Volume) — the proficiency spine. The Companion Volume added mediation, online interaction, and plurilingual/pluricultural descriptors (Council of Europe). It is criterion-referenced (can-do), which matches your mastery-gate philosophy. Caveat (professional consensus): CEFR was built for adults; for young learners its descriptors assume classroom/adolescent cognition. The established solution is a dual ladder: Cambridge Young Learners path (Pre A1 Starters → A1 Movers → A2 Flyers, designed for ages 6–12) as the child-appropriate benchmark (Cambridge English), with CEFR anchoring the system map.
    Understanding by Design (UbD) — the unit-design engine. Backward design (desired results → acceptable evidence → learning plan) is the most defensible way to guarantee your requirement that "every level has clearly established entry requirements, outcomes, assessment criteria, and progression standards" (ASCD UbD white paper).
    UDL 3.0 (CAST, July 2024) — the access layer. Its 3.0 update explicitly emphasizes belonging, learner identity, and joy — directly aligned with your inclusion mandate (udlguidelines.cast.org). UDL is the evidence-based replacement for learning-styles thinking (see §2.2 note 7).
    Webb's DOK — the rigor auditor for tasks and questions. Your own book is the center's in-house authority; its five-step content-first protocol and reverse-coding verification become institutional QA practice.

Method layer (how lessons teach, not what they're built on): CLT remains the professional consensus for communicative competence; TBLT has genuine but nuanced evidence — systematic reviews show positive effects on speaking proficiency, engagement, risk-taking, and linguistic complexity, but the field has explicitly concluded it is "not ripe" for strong relative-effectiveness claims versus task-supported teaching (Boers, 2026; Li, 2016 found task-supported instruction produced stronger target-form gains in direct comparison). Established findings on explicit form-focused instruction (Norris & Ortega, 2000; Spada & Tomita, 2010) show explicit teaching outperforming purely implicit approaches for grammatical accuracy. My recommendation: a principled hybrid — task-driven communicative cycles with explicit, timely focus on form — not dogmatic strong-TBLT. This also matches your DOK book's epistemology: rigorous but honest about what evidence actually shows.

Teacher-level refinement tools (not architecture): Bloom's revised taxonomy, Marzano's frameworks, SOLO — useful inside lesson planning and rubric design; dangerous as parallel curriculum structures (four taxonomies describing one course = alignment chaos; this is exactly the "verb trap" your book corrects for DOK).

Rejected as a design premise: learning styles as learner types. Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008, Psychological Science in the Public Interest) found no controlled experimental evidence that matching instruction to a learner's preferred style improves outcomes (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26162104). Your Section 5 already anticipated this — I confirm it and will build on multimodal instruction, UDL accommodations, and cognitive-load management instead. We will not administer "learning style inventories" to students; the instruments lack validity, and labeling a child "a visual learner" is exactly the kind of unearned certainty your Episode 9 ("confidence is not knowledge") warns against.

The single highest-leverage addition to your list: formative assessment as a system (Black & Wiliam, 1998, Inside the Black Box — development of formative assessment practice produces some of the largest achievement gains in the education literature, their reported range 0.4–0.7 SD; full text). It is also the cheapest intervention on this list. Your assessment architecture should be formative-first, summative-light, and criterion-referenced.
2.3 Grouping and placement: what the evidence and the industry say

     Industry practice: intensive English programs worldwide (including the Philippine academy sector) group core instruction by proficiency, because proficiency is the operative variable in what can be taught to whom. International schools group by age with support tiers, because they run a fixed academic curriculum.
     The child-appropriate proficiency ladder exists: Cambridge YLE (Pre A1 Starters / A1 Movers / A2 Flyers) was designed specifically for children ~6–12, "fun, colourful and activity based" — it solves the "CEFR for kids" problem (same source as above).
     Class size: the most credible causal evidence is Tennessee STAR (Finn & Achilles, 1999; Mosteller, 1995) — substantial benefits of small classes in early grades, strongest for disadvantaged/minority students. General effect sizes are modest (Hattie's d≈0.21), but for language classes specifically, the arithmetic of speaking time per student dominates: in a 60-minute class, a student in a group of 16 gets ~4 minutes of individual speaking time; in a group of 8, ~8 minutes; 1:1, the whole hour. This is why the Philippine market invented the 1:1 model — and why we must treat class size as a pedagogical variable, not a convenience.
     Safeguarding and law constrain radical mixing: an 8-year-old in a class with 40-year-olds is a welfare and regulatory problem, not just a pedagogical one (see §2.5 on SSP/minors). Full analysis with my recommendation is Decision 1 in Part 4.

2.4 Time and intensity: the evidence

     The "10-minute attention span" is a neuromyth. Wilson & Korn (2007, "Attention during Lectures: Beyond Ten Minutes," Educational Psychology Review) reviewed the primary studies and found little empirical support for a fixed 10–15 minute decay curve (abstract). Lesson design should engineer attention through activity variety and transitions, not build around an assumed decay clock. (Younger children still genuinely need shorter blocks and movement — the differentiator is developmental appropriateness, not a mythical universal timer.)
     Spacing beats massing. Cepeda et al. (2006, meta-analysis of 254 studies): distributed practice produces substantially better retention than massed practice (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566), with language-learning applications explicitly confirmed in the follow-on literature. Design implication: daily frequency beats session length. Five 60-minute sessions across a week outperform one 5-hour Sunday marathon by a wide margin.
     1:1 tutoring is powerful — but not 2-sigma magic. Bloom's (1984) two-sigma result came from mastery-learning + tutoring under ideal laboratory conditions (Bloom 1984). The modern RCT meta-analysis (Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan, 2020) finds an average tutoring effect of ≈0.37 SD — strong, real, and worth designing around, but with diminishing returns and fatigue at high daily doses (Education Next synthesis).
     The Philippine "Sparta" model is a market reality and a pedagogical risk. Verified current market practice: 8–12 classes/day, compulsory evening self-study, curfews, English-only rules (2026 market guide; English Fella's Sparta track: 8h/day classes + 8h assigned library study). It sells — Korean and Japanese agents demand visible intensity. But it sits poorly against the spacing evidence, sleep-consolidation research, your own Episode 14 (against accelerated consumption of knowledge), and student-attrition reality. This is a strategic fork, and I take a position below.
     Guided-learning-hours as planning guides: Cambridge's published GLH figures (roughly: A2 Key ≈ 180–200 GLH, B1 ≈ 350–400 cumulative, etc.) are planning guides, not laws — CEFR deliberately does not prescribe hours. We will define hours per level empirically and publish them transparently.

PART 4 — THE MAJOR DECISIONS: Evidence, Options, Recommendations
Decision 1 — Grouping and placement: age, proficiency, or both?

Options:

     (A) Proficiency-only (the PH academy default): pure efficiency; ignores development, content sensitivity, and safeguarding; a 9-year-old in an adult A2 class is unacceptable even when "proficient enough."
     (B) Age-only (the school default): pedagogically coherent for a curriculum school; wrong for language acquisition, where a Grade 4 student and a Grade 10 student at A1 need fundamentally different materials, pace, and interaction — and holding a B1-capable 9-year-old in A1 classes is exactly the neglect you forbid.
     (C) Age × proficiency matrix (my recommendation): proficiency is the primary axis for language instruction; age is the primary axis for pedagogy, welfare, and grouping boundaries. Three age bands — Young Learners (≈7–12), Teens (13–17), Adults (18+) — each running the same CEFR ladder with band-specific materials, lesson design, and assessment formats. A YL A2 class and an Adult A2 class both target A2 can-dos, with utterly different classrooms. Placement into a level is by multi-signal assessment (grammar/vocab + listening/reading + timed writing + oral interview; play-based assessment for young children), with a week-2 settling window allowing re-placement at no cost — because no first-day test is perfect, and the honest institution corrects itself.

Edge cases, handled explicitly:

     Young learner with advanced proficiency: stays in the age band (social development, safeguarding, content appropriateness), and receives the challenge through horizontal enrichment (deeper tasks at the same topic, DOK 3–4 extensions, leadership roles, 1:1 acceleration) plus external benchmark targets (YLE → A2 Key → B1 Preliminary). Never seated in an adult class. This resolves your "no student held back" without sacrificing "developmentally appropriate."
     Older learner with beginner English: adults and teens never share core classes with children; an adult beginner is normal (the majority of the market, in fact) and gets an adult A0 strand. The stigma risk is managed by framing (entry diagnostics are universal; adults expect to start at zero).
     Mid-course drift: progress monitoring triggers re-placement within a level's sub-strands rather than waiting for gate points.

Evidence basis: proficiency as the operative instructional variable (intensive-program practice worldwide); Cambridge YLE as the child-appropriate ladder; safeguarding constraints (AUK under-18 standards; BI minors' SSP rules); STAR on small classes for the young bands.

Guided-learning-hour tables (Cambridge) give level-cost anchors, not laws.

Class size bands (deliberate, published): 1:1 tutorials; 1:4–1:8 core language classes; 1:10–1:16 task/project and lecture-format classes. Teacher-student ratios and speaking-time arithmetic are marketing assets ("in our A2 group classes you speak X minutes per hour — here is the math").

Intensity stance (a strategic choice): I recommend against the 10-hour sparta format even though agents ask for it. The evidence (spacing, diminishing returns of massed practice, consolidation) and your own Episode 14 argue for high daily frequency, moderate daily volume, structured evening consolidation, and English-environment rules — "rigorous but humane." This positioning differentiates you from Cebu/Baguio incumbents rather than imitating them, and it is defensible to parents and agents with citations. If the market later forces a sparta SKU, offer it as a distinct track with explicit fatigue-management design — never as the default identity.

Hours per level: define empirically at P2 (e.g., adult intensive A0→A1 ≈ 100–120 guided hours + monitored independent practice), published openly per level — part of the academic-transparency differentiator.
Decision 3 — Delivery model: 1:1, group, or mixed?

The Philippine market's signature is the 1:1 "man-to-man" class; it is also the sector's blind spot: hours of teacher talk with no peer interaction, no mediation, no collaboration — the exact competencies CEFR 2020 added. Recommendation: a mixed "4+2+1" architecture for the intensive program — 4 × 1:1 tutorials (precision targeting of the four skills), 2 × small-group classes (interaction, task work, mediation), 1 × guided consolidation block. Justification: 1:1 evidence (≈0.37 SD average; Bloom's ceiling conditions), group interaction evidence (TBLT reviews: engagement, complexity, speaking gains), fatigue management, and unit economics. For local learners, a group-first model (1:8) with 1:1 add-ons. This is one of the few places we can out-design the incumbents on both pedagogy and price architecture.
Decision 4 — Progression philosophy: resolving your inclusion/standards tension

Your Section 5 contains two laws in tension: "no student should advance without demonstrating required competencies" and "no student should be unnecessarily held back." Holding both requires a designed mechanism, not slogans. Recommendation: criterion-referenced mastery gates + conditional promotion ("progress with provision") + skip-by-demonstration.

     Gates: each level ends with a gate assessment (integrated performance + skills checks) against published exit competencies — pass, and you advance.
     Conditional: a student who narrowly misses criteria advances on a written bridging plan (4-week targeted intervention + reassessment) rather than repeating a whole level. No punitive traps, no social passing.
     Skip: any student may challenge a gate early through demonstrated performance (portfolio + gate exam) — the DiplomatiQ "unlock the next tier by showing the capacity" model, which you have already built once.

PART 5 — WHERE I CHALLENGE YOUR ASSUMPTIONS

You instructed me to challenge; here are the seven places I would push back:

    The framework list is too long for architecture. CEFR + UbD + UDL + DOK carry the building; CLT/TBLT is the method layer; Bloom/Marzano/SOLO are teacher tools. Nine frameworks as co-equal architecture produces exactly the misalignment your DOK book exists to correct. Each framework earns its place by naming its layer — or it doesn't enter.
